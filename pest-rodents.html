Signs to look for

How to tell if you have rodents

  • Droppings along walls, in cabinets, behind appliances or in the attic
  • Gnaw marks on food packaging, wiring, trim or stored items
  • Scratching or scurrying in walls and ceilings, usually at night
  • Shredded insulation, paper or fabric gathered into nesting material
  • Greasy rub marks along baseboards and joists where they run the same route repeatedly
  • Pets fixating on a spot in a wall or under an appliance

Why it matters here

What rodents mean for an Alabama property

They cause damage well beyond the mess

Rodents gnaw constantly to manage their incisors, and wiring, insulation, stored goods and HVAC ducting all take the damage. Chewed wiring in an attic is a fire risk, not a nuisance.

They are a contamination problem

Droppings and urine contaminate insulation, pantry goods and food-prep surfaces. For a commercial kitchen or warehouse, it is also an inspection failure waiting to happen.

Populations grow fast

Mice reach breeding age in weeks. A problem noticed in autumn, when they move indoors for warmth, is usually bigger than it looks by the time anyone hears scratching.

Before we get there

What you can do yourself

None of this replaces treatment, but it reduces the pressure and makes the work hold longer.

  • Store pet food and birdseed in sealed metal or hard plastic containers, not bags
  • Trim tree limbs back from the roof, since roof rats use them as a bridge
  • Clear wood piles, dense ground cover and clutter from against the structure
  • Fit door sweeps and cover foundation vents with proper hardware cloth rather than screen
  • Take care with droppings: ventilate the area, avoid sweeping or vacuuming them dry, and wear gloves

Questions

Rodents questions we get asked

I caught one mouse. Am I done?

Usually not. Mice are social and breed quickly, so a single catch is more often the first one than the last one. The inspection is what tells you which it is.

Why do they keep coming back every winter?

Because the entry points are still open. Cold weather changes their motivation, not their access. Exclusion is what breaks the annual cycle.

Do you handle squirrels, raccoons and bats?

We handle rodent and wildlife work on structures, and the first step is the same: identify the animal and find the opening. Bats in particular have legal protections and seasonal restrictions, so tell us if you think that is what you have.
